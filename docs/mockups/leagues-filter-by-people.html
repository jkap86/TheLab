Manager tool · /manager/[searched] · four options

Filter leagues by who's in them

Two new questions for the Leagues tab: which of my leagues hold this player, and which do I share with this manager. Everything below is the same control seated four different ways — the choice is where it lives, not what it is.

The one finding that shapes all four

Read first

A person is not a rule. Every filter in the dialog today is a key, a comparison and a number — qb+sf ≥ 2, rec = 0.5 — and the rule row exists because all three parts are choices. A player or a leaguemate is just a name, out of several hundred, and there is nothing to compare it to. So the control that fits is a search that leaves tokens behind, not a third rule bay with three menus in every row.

That is what all four options draw. What separates them is the seat — and the seat decides how much chrome it costs, how discoverable it is, and whether the shared dialog has to learn about a manager.

Needs a lookup

The predicate is pure over one league

matchesFilters reads a league's own settings, and the trades board runs it over a whole season. "Owns Bijan" is rosters[league_id]; "shared with" is members[league_id]. Neither is on the league. Either the predicate takes a context the trades page passes empty, or this is a second narrowing layer inside the manager tool, after the shared one.

Costs a request

Leagues fetches neither route today

Rosters and membership are the other two tabs' resources. The query cache is shared per manager, so a reader who has opened Players or Leaguemates already pays nothing — a cold Leagues tab pays one request per list the control offers. Gate the fetch on the control being opened, exactly as the ADP board is gated on its drawer.

any is real here

The one list that earns a match mode

House rule: league rules narrow on an attribute, so they all AND. This is a set of subjects — and "leagues where I have Bijan or Chase" is asked as often as "leagues where I have both". Same argument the trades selection makes. Default all, as it does.

Why it fits

One dialog holds the whole selection, so the readout rail's count, chips and breakdown keep being the single place a reader audits what they narrowed to — which is the rail's whole job. The draft-and-Apply model is already right for this: the per-name counts beside each result can't be read while the list behind the dialog moves.

What it costs

The shared dialog learns about a manager. It renders on the trades page too, so it takes an optional subjects prop that page passes nothing for — a shared component with a half nobody on one of its two pages can use. The panel also grows by ~150px, and it is already scrolling at 72vh on a laptop.

Why it fits

It is the cheapest thing to build and the least new vocabulary: the row, the float, the one-open-at-a-time rule, the outside-press dismiss and the Escape ordering all exist. Two rows cost ~62px closed, against a bay's ~150px, and the trough is what a reader already scans for a one-line answer to "what is this set to".

What it costs

A row states one selection. Several players means either a row that reads "3 players" — the one place in the dialog that hides its own state — or tokens spilling out of a control built for a single line. And it puts a subject beside three attributes, which quietly says they behave alike, when only this one has a match mode.

C

Its own key on the plate, beside Filters

Not in the dialog at all. A second key in the header plate's bottom edge opens a search panel over the list: type a name, press a result, it becomes a token. The dialog stays about what a league is; this asks who is in it.

Why it fits

It commits live and shows its answer in the plate's own count as you type, so finding "leagues where I have Bijan" is one press and four keystrokes — no dialog, no Apply. It also keeps the shared modal free of manager-only data, and both filter sets stay named for the question they ask, the way Leagues and Filters do on the trades board.

What it costs

A third control on a pinned plate, and the second one to wear a badge — the plate's edge is the height budget the record readout already fought for. Live commit also means no per-result count read against a still list; the counts here are each name's own total, not what it would leave beside the rest of the selection.

D

Start it from the share cards — pairs with any of the above

Not a filter control at all. Every share card already lists the leagues behind it; the one thing it can't do is take you there. A single action per row sets the subject and drops you on Leagues with it applied — so the question is answered where it is actually asked.

Why it fits

The share card is where a reader is already looking at "19 of 116" and wanting the 19. It costs no new filter surface, no search, and no menu to populate — the name is the row. It also makes whichever control you build discoverable, since the first time most readers see the filter is the moment it fills itself in.

What it costs

On its own it is one subject at a time and no way to add a second, or to reach a player you can't already see — so it is an entry point rather than the feature. It needs a control behind it to hold and edit what it set, which is why it pairs rather than competes.

What I'd build

C, with D as the way in. The two questions this adds are subjects, not attributes, and the app already draws that line twice — the trades board separates which leagues from which trades into two controls for exactly this reason. A key of its own keeps the shared dialog free of manager data, keeps live commit (which is what makes the search feel like a search), and lets the share cards fill it in.

If the plate's edge can't take a second key, A is the next best — it keeps one place to audit a selection, at the cost of the shared modal carrying a half the trades page can't use.

  1. State beside the league filters. A SubjectFilters in the manager layout's provider, reset per manager by the same subtree key — so the selection survives the trip between the three tabs.
  2. Narrowing after the shared predicate, in useFilteredLeagues, where the lookups are in hand. features/shared stays free of anything a trades reader can't use.
  3. Data on open — the players and leaguemates payloads, gated on the control being opened, off the same cache the other two tabs fill.
  4. It narrows all three tabs, which is a feature: Players then answers "who else do I own alongside Bijan", Leaguemates "who am I in those leagues with". Expect the filtered player's own share to read 19 of 19 — correct, and worth a note rather than a fix.
Drawn in the app's own material — .lab-chip keys, .lab-well troughs, the cyan active token, Orbitron on the named rows. Single-theme on purpose: this is a mockup of a dark instrument, and it would misreport itself on a light ground. Counts are illustrative.
