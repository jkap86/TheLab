Three bays over a board that scrolls

The ADP drawer's pinned block is four parts stacked — identity, window, filters, curve — and it takes 314 of the panel's 660 pixels before a single player is listed. Nine rows of board. Turning the bottom three into an accordion is the ask; here are four ways to build it, rendered live. Press the bays and watch the board under each one move.

Every panel measures itself — the meter under each is the real pinned-block height and what it leaves, recomputed on every press. Which is how the first thing worth knowing turned up: an accordion with the window open is 43px taller than the block is today, because the two shut rows sit above the same panel. The win is at rest and it is large (15 rows against 9); the loss is while the window is open. Everything below is a way of arranging that one trade.

Reach for a disclosure when what is behind it is genuinely settings; a control that has to be summarised outside itself is one that wanted to be on the page. CLAUDE.md — arrived at when the trades filters lost their ledge
The window is the board's population, so setting one is the first thing the drawer is opened for. That is why it was pulled out of a collapse and seated permanently — the collapse's arithmetic was never wrong; what it left out is what was being collapsed. CLAUDE.md — the lookback panel's own note

So the accordion is not free: it re-collapses the one control that was deliberately expanded. What makes it defensible is that the failure it caused the first time was a row of presets beside the trigger that made the window look answered — a bay carrying the live window and its own density picture says the opposite. Every option below states each bay's value on its closed row, because a bay that doesn't is the ledge mistake again.

01Option A — Stacked bays

The literal accordion. Three collapsed rows, one open at a time, the open one expanding in place so the board resizes under it. Pressing an open bay closes it, which gives a fourth state the current block has no way to reach: everything shut, the board at full height.

A · one open, in place

Press a bay to open it; press it again to shut everything.

02Option B — Bay rail

Not an accordion but its close cousin: one rail of three keys and a single body under them, sized once to the tallest bay. The block never changes height, so the board never moves at all — the property the current design has and every accordion gives up.

03Option C — Floating bays

The rows stay one line each, always three; the open bay hangs over the board instead of pushing it. This is the app's own grammar — the league filters dialog collapsed its three segment groups exactly this way, and the note on it says the float is the load-bearing half.

04Option D — Two bays and a live curve

Option A, with one refinement: the curve bay's closed row is the control. A slider is already one line, so there is nothing behind the press worth the press — put it on the row and the third bay never opens or closes. Still three sections, still in the asked-for order, and the one control that must be movable-while-watching is never behind a disclosure.

05Verdict

D, falling back to A

C is out on its own argument. The drawer's shape exists so that changing a setting and watching the board move is one glance; a bay that floats over the board is the only option here that breaks it, and it breaks it worst for the curve, whose entire reason for being a slider is the board bending underneath.

B measured its way out. Constant height is genuinely valuable — and today's block already has it, so the only thing left for a rearrangement to buy is board. B buys 9px, because a body sized to the window means the two short bays pay the window's height and show nothing in it. It gives up the density picture and the window's caption for that, which is a worse readout for the same board.

A and D are the same design, and the difference is whether the curve gets a body. It doesn't need one: a slider is already one line, and behind a press it becomes a control you open to use and close to see the effect of. D keeps all three sections in the asked-for order, holds the board at 14 rows at rest against today's 9, and leaves the window as the one bay that costs real height — which it should, being the one bay that is a real instrument.

The honest cost, in both: with the window open the block is taller than it is today (364px against 314), so the board is 7 rows while a window is being set. That is the right way round — the reader setting a window is looking at the window — but it is the reason the window bay should be the one that opens by default and the one that remembers being shut.

Three things to hold to whichever way it goes:

  1. A closed bay states its value, or this is the trades ledge again. Leagues says the rule count and the draft kind; the window says its own label and carries the density as a sparkline; the curve says what it does to a board.
  2. Cyan means narrowed, not open. The value on a closed row goes accent only when that bay is doing something to the board — the same test adpNarrowingCount already makes for the app-bar trigger's bars.
  3. The window opens by default on a cold drawer. It is the first thing the drawer is opened for; a reader who has never set one should meet the instrument, not a row saying "All of 2026". Once a window is set, the bay remembers being shut.
