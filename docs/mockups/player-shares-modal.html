Leagues tab · the shares browse · three sheets

Player shares, opened as a board

The Player shares key on the heading rail opens a 26rem panel today: one column of names, a count each, capped at 60vh. The ask is to promote it to what the Players tab already is — a list with the league filters over it and four pickable stat columns — floating over the leagues page as a translucent sheet. Three seatings below, differing on one question the size creates.

What the sheet is replacing, and the problem it inherits

Read first

The browse is already the right idea: the players worth narrowing by are the ones held everywhere, so the panel shows every rostered player most-held first and a press leaves the same token a typed search would. What it can't do is say anything about a player. A name and a number is the table the Players tab was before its columns were pickable — and that tab is one click away carrying exposure, record, win rate, league mix and ADP for exactly these rows.

Promoting it costs one thing, and every option below is an answer to it: a sheet over most of the screen covers the list it is narrowing. The panel is small on purpose — press a name and the rows behind it move while you watch, which is what makes committing live safe without an Apply button. At 90% of the viewport that feedback is underneath the thing giving it.

Translucency is a new material here, so spend it on the frame. Everything in this app is a lit metal face on an opaque ground; glass is not in the vocabulary yet. All three sheets are translucent at the edge — the blur is what says the page is still there — and all three keep the rows themselves in an opaque well. Four numbers a row, read over a hundred league cards drifting behind them, is the one thing the effect cannot be allowed to buy.

What it buys

Almost nothing is drawn twice. The sheet's body is ManagerPlayers' body — same rail, same cards, same column state — so the browse and the tab stay one thing by construction rather than by discipline. The count in the bar says 37 of 84, and the glass says the page is still there without spending a pixel on saying so, which is what keeps a sheet this size from reading as a place you navigated to.

What it costs

A share card is 62px. Eighty per cent of a laptop viewport is about eight of them — the same eight the capped panel shows today, at four times the pixels, which is a hard thing to defend for a list several hundred rows long. And the see-through only half pays for itself: what a reader can actually watch narrow is the band around the sheet, since the rows themselves have to sit on an opaque well to stay readable. Blur it too little and the league names compete with the player names; too much and there is nothing back there to see.

State it

Don't try to see the answer — print it. The sheet is a console: dense rows under the same four headings, and a rail down the right carrying the filters key, what is narrowing, the live count with its meter, and what the survivors are. The filters dialog's own readout, borrowed for the selection it was built to describe.

What it buys

The space goes where the list is. Eighteen rows at a glance is the difference between browsing the shares and reading them, which is the whole reason the browse exists uncapped. Nothing is read through moving content, so the sheet can be as opaque as it needs to be — the rail is the feedback, and it is the same rail the filters dialog already answers with, chips and meter and breakdown.

What it costs

A dense row is a third grain for a share: the cards' meters, the expansion listing the leagues behind a player, and the position-coloured pills all shrink to text. Two lists that show the same rows in two shapes is exactly the drift ShareList was extracted to stop. And 186px of the sheet's width is rail rather than list.

C

Don't cover it

The sheet is seated under the manager plate rather than over it. The plate stays lit and uncovered, carrying what it already carries — the count, the record over the leagues left, the scope line, and the filters key in its own corner. So the answer to "what did that do" is where a reader already reads it, and the sheet has no readout of its own to build.

What it buys

The one control that has to be reachable — the filters key — is already outside the sheet, in the corner it was machined into, so "the filters button in it" costs no new seat and opens no dialog over a dialog. The plate's own numbers do the console rail's job for free: the count, the record summed over the leagues left, and the scope line naming both halves of the selection in words, all moving as you pick.

What it costs

It is the least modal of the three, which cuts both ways: most of the screen becomes most of the screen below the header, about 10 rows, and the sheet has to know the plate's height — a measurement that goes stale the moment the plate grows a line. It also leaves the filters key visually outside the thing it now filters, which is only obvious if you already know the plate is the header for both.

The two keys, and where what they open goes

Applies to A and B

Both things the sheet has to carry are already dialogs: LeagueFiltersModal is a 1040px panel, and the columns editor is another one — four slot previews, the catalogue in bays, the preset row. Opened from inside a sheet, each is a modal over a modal of nearly its own size, which reads as a stack of sheets rather than as a control being used. Two answers, and they can be taken separately for each key.

True of all three

It commits live — there is no Apply

A press narrows the leagues immediately, exactly as the rail's tokens do now. There is no draft to protect, because a row's count states how many leagues hold that player, which is true whatever else is picked.

One selection, two doors

A player picked in the sheet leaves the same token a typed search leaves, and the input stays live over the list. Opening the sheet is a different door onto the control, not a second control.

The columns are the tab's columns

The same persisted share selection ManagerPlayers uses. Aim a column in the sheet and the Players tab is already aimed — one board, seen twice.

The counts follow the filters

Every row's share is out of the leagues the other filters leave, which is why the filters key belongs in here at all: narrowing to dynasty rewrites all 981 numbers, and the list reorders under them.

The rows stay in an opaque well

The glass is the frame. Four numbers a row read over a drifting league list is the one thing the material can't be allowed to cost.

The shares wait only on rosters

The leaguemate list is the search door's other half and has no business holding this one up — the rule the panel already keeps.

If it were mine: C, with B's rail as the fallback

C is the cheapest thing that answers the whole ask, and it is cheap for a reason worth stating: the plate is already the header of the list, so a sheet seated under it inherits a readout, a record, a scope line and a filters key without building any of them. Nothing opens over anything except the columns editor, which is the one dialog that genuinely belongs on top of the list it aims.

A is the one to build if the answer is "it should feel like the Players tab" — it is that tab, over the leagues page, and almost no new code. But eight rows is the same eight the panel shows today, so the sheet would be spending a lot of screen to change how the browse looks rather than how much of it you can read.

B is right if the shares list is going to grow teeth — sorting, a position filter, more than four columns. Its rail is the honest answer to a sheet that covers the page, and 18 rows is what "most of the screen" is actually for. It costs a second row grain, which is the thing to weigh: ShareList exists because the two share views had already drifted once.

  1. Pick the seating — the question is really "how much of the leagues list does the reader need while picking", and A / B / C are see it, read it, keep it.
  2. Then the two keys — nested dialogs (nothing to build, one dismissal rule to fix) or trays (one surface, one board drawn twice). They can differ per key.
  3. Then the leaguemates door — the same sheet with the ADP columns dropped and the avatar in the first cell is the leaguemate shares, free. Worth deciding now, since it is what stops the sheet from being a player-only part.
