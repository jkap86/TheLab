Trades board · the card · four treatments

The values are the quietest thing on a card about value

Two complaints, and they turn out to be one. The numbers are set in the body face at the smallest size on the card, so the page's whole subject reads last — and a side plate is built like a card, so the card it is inside stops reading as an object. Both are hierarchy: the card is not more of a thing than its parts are, and the value is not more of a thing than the names around it. Four ways to fix it, each shown at the width the complaint came from.

What is actually going wrong

The numbers are the quietest type on the card

The league name is Orbitron at 13px, uppercase, tracked. The manager's name is 13px bold. The value — the thing the page is for — is 12px bold body face at 85%, and the per-asset numbers are 11px at 60%. The card's subject is priced lowest in its own hierarchy.

Cost

A side is built exactly like a card

Raised, walled, brushed, rounded, with an avatar, a name and a readout on it. That is the card's own construction one step down — and one step is not enough to read as containment, so on a phone the list looks like eight manager plates rather than four trades.

Read

The spacing is already right, which is the tell

8px between sides against roughly 32px between the nearest plates of two cards is a 4:1 ratio, and it still doesn't read. When proximity is correct and grouping still fails, the problem is weight — so the fix has to be material, and more air alone will not buy it.

A

One face, two zones

Delete the side plate. A side becomes a region of the card's own face, and what parts two of them is a seam milled across the full width. The card is then the only object in the list — nothing inside it is built like a card, because nothing inside it is built at all.

Why it fits

It fixes the hierarchy at the root rather than compensating for it. A side stopped looking like a card because it stopped being an object: no wall, no bloom, no rounded box, no second lit face. On the phone, where the sides stack, the list becomes four cards instead of eight plates you have to pair up.

The seam is the app's own device — the parting line already under the manager row, run full-bleed. A cut that reaches both edges reads as machining in one part; a rounded box reads as a second part.

The value goes to the accent, on the display face, lit inside its lens. It becomes the brightest thing on the card, which is what a page called trades priced by a column called value is for.

What it costs

The take/give grammar loses one level of contrast. The give groove used to be a cut in a lit plate; now it is a cut in the card face, so the drop from take to give is smaller. It survives (the groove is still darker than the face) but it is worth checking on a real haul.

A lit cyan number per side is two accents per card and up to fifty on screen. The app's own rule is that boldness gets spent in one place — if this reads as a christmas tree, the answer is A's structure with D's engraved numerals.

Cheapest to build: deletes classes
B

A chassis band and a ledger

Give the card a full-bleed recessed header spanning edge to edge, and seat the sides in a trough milled into the face. Nothing inside a card can look like that band, so the band is unmistakably where a card begins. The lines then rule out to their numbers the way a ledger does.

Why it fits

The band is the single most legible "a card starts here" mark available, because it is the one shape the card's contents never take. You do not have to compare weights to find the boundary — you look for the dark stripe.

The leader dots do something the current card does not: they tie each name to its number across the gap, so four numbers stop reading as a loose column. On this data they also visibly sum — 2,726 + 4,171 + 2,047 is 8,944 — which is the one thing a reader most wants to check and currently has to do in their head.

The sides keep their plates, so nothing about the take/give grammar or the seated-part material has to be re-argued.

What it costs

The trough costs about 16px of width and 16px of height per card, which over a windowed list of forty thousand is real scrolling.

Dotted leaders are texture, and this card already has a groove, a brushed face and a specular sweep. At 13px they are close to the noise floor — worth testing at the smallest size before committing.

The lines only sum when the metric covers every one of them. Jonas Sanker is unpriced, so WiscoMick's column does not add up and the leader invites the reader to try. That needs the total's hover to keep saying 3 of 4 priced.

Costs ~16px of width per card
C

Head to head

Run a milled spine between the two sides and let it carry the difference. The sides are then physically joined — you cannot read them as two cards, because something bridges them. The value becomes a score: Orbitron at 17px in a sunken well, one per side.

Why it fits

Separation by connection rather than by containment. The spine only makes sense between two things, so a reader never has to judge which plates belong together — the part between them says so.

It is the most decorative answer by a distance, and it answers a question the card currently makes you do arithmetic for: which side came out ahead, and by how much.

The big numeral gives the side head a shape at a glance. Scrolling past, you read two scores and a name, which is roughly how anyone actually skims a trade feed.

What it costs

The delta is a new claim, and the app has been careful not to make it. It is only honest when both sides priced everything they took — KTC covers about 500 players, so an unpriced rookie or a kicker makes the difference a lie. The second card here shows the fallback: no number, just the exchange mark.

A three-way trade has no spine and no delta — three sides do not have a difference — so the card has two layouts rather than one, and the three-way falls back to something closer to A.

17px numerals plus a 26px spine is the tallest option here, on the page whose whole performance story is how many cards fit in a window.

Tallest card; two layouts to maintain
D

A nameplate on the edge

Sink the league name half into the card's top edge, the way the manager plate's filters key rides its bottom edge. A part rising out of a card is the strongest "one object" mark there is, and it costs no interior height. Inside, the sides lose their wall and become flush inlays; the value is engraved rather than lit.

Why it fits

The nameplate reads as a card's own label, so the top edge is found before anything inside is read — and it is free vertically, since it occupies margin the list was already spending as a gap.

It is the app's existing device, not a new one. The manager header already has a part straddling its edge, and re-using that grammar is what keeps the trades page from reading as a different product.

Engraved numerals are decoration without a second accent: the number is cut into the plate rather than lit on it, so fifty of them on screen stay calm. This is the option that scales to a long scroll.

What it costs

It is the quietest of the four. If the complaint is really "this is not decorative enough", D under-delivers on its own and wants A's lit lens for the total.

The overhang has to sit inside the measured box. TradesList measures each item and carries the gap as padding inside it, so the nameplate's margin must be part of that box or every card below it drifts.

Flush inlays are a third material between raised and recessed. It works here, but it is a new word in a vocabulary the codebase documents carefully, and it wants writing down.

Free vertically; smallest visual change

Pick A, and take D's numerals if it reads hot

A is the only option that fixes the cause rather than compensating for it: a side stops looking like a card because it stops being an object. It is also the smallest diff — it deletes classes — and the cheapest to scroll. Its one risk is two lit cyan numbers per card over a long list, and the hedge for that is already drawn here: keep A's structure and swap in D's engraved numeral. B is the right answer if the boundary has to be findable without comparing anything at all, and C is the right answer if this page should be a scoreboard — but C's delta is a claim KTC cannot always support, and that is a product decision before it is a design one.

True whichever is chosen

Constraints

The em dash never becomes a zero

However decorative the numeral gets, an unpriced player is — and a side that priced part of itself still says so. Jonas Sanker is on every card above for exactly that reason: KTC prices about 500 dynasty skill players, so a blank is a fact about the board and a zero would be a claim about the player.

Orbitron is wider, so a number on it steps down a size

The same rule the league rows already follow — text-sm on the display face where the body face was 15px. Every option above sets the line values at 10–10.5px against the current 11px, and keeps tabular-nums so a column of them still lines up.

The gap between cards has to beat the gap inside one

It already does on the geometry — 8px between sides against roughly 32px between the nearest plates of two cards. That is why the fix here is material and not air. Every option still nudges CARD_GAP from 12px to 18–22px, because it is free and it helps.

Whatever the card grows, the virtualizer measures it

TradesList measures each card and carries the inter-card gap as padding inside the measured box. A nameplate on the edge, a taller spine or a trough are all fine — as long as they are inside that box, and not a margin outside it.

The picker stays in the header, never on a card

The value column is a list-wide selection, so the control that aims it belongs above the list. None of these options put a menu on a card, and forty thousand cards each holding one is the reason.

A three-way still has to draw

Some leagues run them, and the odd side already takes the full row rather than leaving an empty cell. A and D handle it unchanged; B needs the trough to hold three; C has no spine for it and falls back.
