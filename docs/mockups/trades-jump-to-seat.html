Trades · where the board begins

One instrument, four seats

The jump-to key floats over the board’s trailing edge, 34px square, and lands squarely on a card’s timestamp plate. The part is too small and the seat is on top of something. Below: the same instrument drawn once at a size that reads, and the four places it could sit — two of which cover nothing at any scroll depth, and two of which buy back the reach that pinning it was for.

Every board here is the real page at 864px (PageShell width="wide"), with the 390px column beside the seats whose behaviour depends on the row wrapping.

What it does today

the complaint

The key is h-0 sticky over the list — it takes no space in the flow, which is what makes it reachable a thousand rows down, and what it pays for that is coverage. On this board the top-right corner is a card’s instant ledge: a plate at exactly the same corner, so the two read as one broken part rather than as stacked ones.

the collision

Whatever card the scroll has parked under the key loses its timestamp. It is transient — a different card each time — which is worse than permanent, because it reads as a rendering bug rather than as a part.

the size

34px of glyph with an 8.5px plate under it. It was drawn that small because it floats: a bigger floating part covers more. The size and the seat are one decision, so they have to move together.

what the seat bought

Reach. The board is a hundred thousand rows deep and the moment you want to travel is the moment the controls are three screens up. Any seat that gives that up has to be worth it — which is what B and D are for.

The instrument

drawn once, seated four ways

Same job, four times the presence: a rail rather than a key. The date is engraved into a .lab-readout cut at 16px display face — the size the countdown’s digits are drawn at, and the first thing on the row a reader lands on. Beside it, the two presses the question actually has: a step either way by a day, Today back to the newest, and the calendar key that opens the same native picker as now.

the date is the part

Today an icon says a control exists and a 8.5px plate says where the board is. Here the date is the readout, at a size a reader takes in without aiming — which is the whole of “more prominent”.

absent, not zero

At the top of the board there is no bound, so the readout says so in words and the rail goes out. A date reading “today” would put a bound on screen the query string deliberately doesn’t carry.

two new presses

The step keys are free at this width and are the press a reader travelling by a day actually wants — today that is the calendar, three taps deep. The later step is inert at the newest, drawn flat rather than hidden.

A

Board head — seated above the first card

The rail is the top of the board, in normal flow, between the controls and the first card. It covers nothing, ever, at any depth — and it scrolls away with everything else above the list.

What it is

One part, full column width, in the element the virtualizer already watches — so it costs nothing new: a band that changes height moves the list down, and the observer on the page header is how the list is told.

It reads as a masthead for the list rather than as a control over it, which is exactly what it is: this board begins on June 30 and runs older downwards. That sentence has never been on screen.

What it costs

Reach. Travelling from four hundred rows down means scrolling back to the top — which is what the pinned key was built to avoid, and the one thing this seat cannot answer.

~52px of first-paint height above the first card, on a page that spent ~96px deleting its own masthead to get the cards up.

B

Pinned board head — the same rail, held under the app bar

Identical part, identical seat at the top; it stays when the page scrolls. It never covers a card’s corner, because it spans the whole column and the list passes beneath it the way the list already passes beneath the app bar.

What it is

The manager tool’s own construction: a card pinned under the bar, offset by --site-header-h, painting the page ground so rows don’t show through it. This page already has the machinery — the seek wrapper is sticky today, it is just h-0 and 34px wide.

Reach is kept whole, and it gains something the floating key never had: at any depth the band states where the board begins. Right now a reader four hundred cards down has nothing on screen saying the board is bounded at all.

What it costs

A band of viewport, permanently — ~52px of every screen, on a page whose content is a windowed list. That is the honest reading of “covers nothing”: it covers no part, and it does take a strip of the list, exactly as the manager header does.

Two pinned bands stack (app bar + this), which on a 667px phone is ~110px of chrome before a card.

C

In the controls trough — a third row beside the scope

The seek goes back where it came from, but drawn as an instrument rather than as a labelled date field: the circle stepper’s own grammar, one row down. Nothing new is pinned, nothing new is built.

What it is

The cheapest of the four and the most consistent: the trough already holds a stepper with a lit rail and an engraved readout, so a second one under it reads as the same instrument panel rather than as a new kind of part.

It also puts the two things a reader sets — which trades and from when — in one place, which is the argument the seek was moved out on, read the other way.

What it costs

The same reach as A, plus the argument that lost last time: a position is not a setting, and filed with the settings it reads as one. The board’s own bound sits inside a block about filters.

It is also the least prominent of the four — one row of a three-row block rather than a part with ground around it.

D

Board header — the pinned band carries the count too

B’s seat, earning its viewport: the band is not only the seek, it is the board’s own header — how many trades, and where they begin. The count leaves the controls block, so the pinned strip is paid for by something already on the page.

What it is

The count is the number every filter on this page is pressed to move, and it currently scrolls away with the controls — so a reader deep in a board has neither the size of it nor where it starts. Both belong to the list rather than to the filters, which is what makes them one part.

The trough loses a cell and gets shorter; the pinned band is net ~30px rather than ~52px, because half of what it holds used to be above it.

What it costs

The same viewport band as B, and one more thing to get right: the count is dimmed while a narrowed board is in flight, so the pinned band has a state that changes under the reader’s eye rather than only when they press.

It is the largest diff of the four — the count moves component, and TradeControls’s trailing group is re-laid around its absence.

Where this lands

The complaint has two halves and they pull apart at exactly one point: making the part bigger is free, and keeping it reachable is not — a control that follows the scroll is always over something, and the only question is whether that something is a card’s corner or a strip of the list.

  1. If “covers nothing” is literal — A. The rail sits above the first card, full width, in the flow. Nothing is ever obscured at any scroll depth, the date is four times the size, and the diff is the smallest of the pinned alternatives: the sticky wrapper simply stops being sticky.
  2. If the reach is worth a band — D. Same rail, held under the app bar, carrying the count so the strip it takes is paid for by something that was already on the page and scrolling away. This is the one that makes a deep board legible: 54,067 trades, beginning June 30, at row 4,000.
  3. B is D without the argument — take it if the count should stay with the filters that move it.
  4. C is the fallback if the pinning should go away entirely: cheapest, most consistent with the block above it, and it re-files a position as a setting, which is the reason it left.

A and D are worth pricing against each other; B and C are each one step off one of them.

Ninth in the series, after trades scope & jump-to — which is where the 34px key and its pinned seat were decided. What is re-opened here is only the seat and the size; the rule the part keeps is the one that mock arrived at, that a date on screen is a claim about where the board begins and an unbounded board must not make one.
