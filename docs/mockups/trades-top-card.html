Trades board · the block above the list · five directions

Four claims about one board, at four weights, in an order the width decides

The top card holds everything true about the board underneath it: which population (the circle), what it means (the sentence), how big it came out (the count), what else is narrowing it (the season and the league rules), and the two keys that move any of it. Five facts and two controls, currently drawn as seven peers in a wrapping row inside a recessed trough — which is the one material on this page that says nothing is here.

Five directions, drawn in the app's own faces over the real board. Each is shown at the trades page's own 864px and at 390px, because the wrapping is most of the argument and it only shows up at the width it happens at. Every height in the compare table is measured off these renders at load rather than typed in. The circle steppers are live — press them.

The material says nothing is here

Every object on this page is machined — the cards are slabs with nameplates riding their edges, the seek is a corner-lit billet, the app bar is keys and wells. The block that describes all of it is a trough: the one face in the vocabulary that means "a recess things are seated in". It reads as leftover chrome above the product.

Two sentences, one question

The whole crawled market. and 2026 · all leagues both describe the population, in the same 12px grey, separated by the count and two keys. One is the circle's gloss and the other the filters' summary — nothing on screen says they are different questions, or that the second one moves when you press Leagues.

The headline is not drawn as one

54,504 is the number every filter on the page is pressed to move and the only feedback a press gives. It is 18px, mid-row, between a grey sentence and two keys, and it dims on stale — so the one thing worth watching is the thing most easily missed.

The height is whatever the width decides

Everything wraps. At 864px it is —; at 390px the rows break four ways and it is —, with the scope line landing last because it is order-last basis-full. That height is charged to every card on a windowed list, and it is not a number anyone chose.

What it buys

The two facts that identify the board hold the two corners, exactly as a trade card's name and instant do — and they are one flex row, so a long circle name truncates against the count rather than against a width guessed ahead of it.

The count stops being mid-row text. On a plate, engraved, at the edge, it is the first thing read and the last thing a scroll takes away.

It is the page's own grammar. A reader scrolling from this block into the first card crosses no material boundary — same wall, same chamfer, same nameplate.

What it costs

Height. A slab pays for its wall and its plate overhang; measured here it is — against today at 864px and — at 390px.

It looks pressable. A slab on this page is a card you press, and this one opens nothing. It has to wear the fixed variant (.lab-slab-fixed, no lift, no bloom) — the rail's own precedent, and easy to lose in a later edit.

The nameplate is now a second place the circle is named, beside the readout. The note has to drop the name and carry only the meaning — which is what it already does.

What it buys

The page finally has a subject. Every filter here is pressed to move one number, and that number is now the largest thing on the block instead of the fourth largest.

The two sentences become one. The circle's name leads the caption and the rules summary sits under it, so what is a gloss and what is a filter summary is settled by position rather than left to a reader to work out.

The readout stops saying what the caption already says. With the name in the caption, the instrument is the rail, the pips and the two steps — ~116px rather than the 304px the aperture needs to hold LEAGUEMATE LEAGUES without truncating. That is what actually buys the single row, and it is the same trade the app bar made when the ADP block dropped its board name for a channel.

It is the cheapest of the four directions that keep a block (— against today at 864px), because the whole block is one row — only E, which keeps no block at all, comes in under it.

What it costs

The phone breaks the thesis, though not the saving. One row needs ~700px; below that the caption and the keys take lines of their own and it is three rows again — — than today, but no longer the single row the direction is named for. "One height at every width" is a claim only the desktop keeps.

The nameless stepper is only legible next to its caption. Move it, shrink the caption, or let the caption wrap away from it and the control becomes four dots and two arrows with nothing on screen saying what they set — which is the state the four keys this instrument replaced could never get into.

A stale board dims the biggest thing on the page. The count already fades while a narrowed board lands — at 34px that flicker is the whole header rather than one small element, and it may need a different treatment (the digits rolling, the way the ADP drawer's draft count does).

It still sits in a trough. This direction changes the hierarchy and not the material — it can be combined with A, which is what the verdict does.

What it buys

Every fact carries its unit. Today 2026 · all leagues is a sentence a reader has to parse; here the season is under season and the rules under rules, so nothing has to be inferred from a separator.

One housing, not five readouts. The card's own argument: five separate cells read as perforation, and housed they are a single object a reader learns the shape of once — and the same shape they already learned one row down.

What is pressable is unambiguous. Everything in the run is sunk; the only raised parts on the block are the stepper and the two keys.

What it costs

The card's own rule says three z-levels. The bezel is a fourth countable depth and is only allowed on a trade card because it is nested inside the third. Repeating it in the header is a second housing in view at the same time, which is exactly what that rule was written to prevent.

The Value gauge and the Value key say the same thing — the gauge reports what the key is set to, a few pixels apart. Either the gauge goes or the key becomes the gauge (which is direction D's rail).

The circle is named twice, in the run and in the readout, and the phone can only afford three gauges — so which three is a decision this direction has to make and today's block never had to.

D

The controls are a rail that pins under the app bar, so at row nine hundred the scope stepper and both keys are still there. The leagues list already learned this: the plate scrolls, the heading rail holds.

What it buys

The scope becomes reachable while reading. It is the page's most consequential narrowing and today it is only reachable by scrolling back to the top — the exact argument that pinned the seek key, applied to the control the seek key was split out from.

The identity can be as generous as it likes, because it is paid for once. A 26px count and a full sentence cost nothing at row nine hundred.

The pinned half is the one part that never wraps, so the height charged to the list while scrolling is fixed by construction.

What it costs

A third pinned thing. The app bar pins, the seek key pins, and this would make three layers of chrome stacked at the top of a windowed board — and the seek key currently floats at the trailing edge exactly where this rail's own keys would end. Those two have to be reconciled before this is buildable.

It pays twice. At rest it is — at 864px, and then the rail is ~46px of permanent coverage on top of the app bar's own — the one cost here that is charged for the whole length of the scroll rather than once at the top.

A sticky part travels only as far as its own parent's box, so the rail cannot be a child of the header block — it has to be a sibling seated in PageShell's <main>, which is the trap the heading rail and the seek key each hit once already.

The key was already a key; what it gains is an edge for its plate to ride.

What it buys

One pinned band instead of two. The app bar, then this — where D would have made three layers and left the seek key floating on top of its own trailing keys. Everything reachable while reading is on one line, and the coverage charged to the board is paid once.

The plate finally rides an edge that is always there. A nameplate is a part rising out of an edge to label what is inside it; today it rises out of a floating key and lands over whichever card the scroll has put beneath it. On the rail's bottom edge it labels the rail — which is exactly what the date is: the band saying what board this is, and where in it you are.

The two questions asked while scrolling become adjacent. "How far out is this drawn" and "where am I standing" are one glance apart rather than at opposite ends of the viewport, and both are one press.

It costs no new material. The rail is .lab-ledge's face, the key is the billet it already is, the plate is .lab-nameplate where it already was.

What it costs

The key stops being at a fixed corner. Floating, it is always at the same place on screen; in the rail it moves with the row — and when the rail wraps at 390px the reader has to find it again. It is kept on the stepper's line here for that reason, which is a rule the phone layout now has to hold.

A wrapped rail has to reserve room for the plate. The date hangs below the stepper's line, so at 390px the row underneath is pushed 13px clear of it — a rule that is invisible in the markup and shows up as a date sitting on a chip the first time someone edits the row.

The rail must publish its own height. Once it wraps, anything measuring the chrome under the bar needs --list-ledge-h's trick — a runtime measurement, which is the app's only one and is worth not acquiring a second of lightly.

It re-opens a seat that was settled recently. The float was itself the fix for the key being filed with the settings, and its resting band above the first card was a second fix on top of that. This is a third move for one control — worth making only because the rail gives it a better edge than the one it has, not because the header wanted the space.

The plate hangs into the board permanently. ~10px of the first visible card, for as long as the reader has travelled — where today's key pays that only while pinned, and the resting band above the list was bought precisely to avoid it.

E

Dissolved — no top card at all

Ask whether the block should exist. The board's identity is a fact about the page, so it goes in the chrome, beside the ADP block that already describes the market. The count and the two triggers join the search bays' own rail, which is a control surface that is already there. What is above the list is then the search and nothing else.

What it buys

The most valuable thing on a windowed board is the height above it, and this is the only direction that gives any back — — at 864px and — at 390px.

The search rail is a control surface that already exists. Putting the count and the two keys on its head makes one instrument out of two blocks that currently sit 12px apart doing the same kind of work.

The circle beside the ADP block is honest. Both describe a population rather than a trade, and the bar's seat is the app's one sanctioned place for a page's own control.

What it costs

The bar's rule is one occupant per route, and the ADP block already holds it. A second part means the seat becomes a group, the wordmark loses its text below sm, and the bar has to be laid out for four parts on a 390px screen.

The sentence has nowhere to live. It is what explains why a step key is inert without an account and what separates the two leaguemate readings — compressed onto one rail line it truncates, which is the failure the readout already had once.

It disperses the board's description across two surfaces. A reader asking "what am I looking at" now looks in the chrome for half the answer and above the list for the other half.

If it were mine: A + B, and D only if the seek key moves

A and B are not really competing — one is the material and the other the hierarchy, and each fixes a different half of the diagnosis. A slab with a nameplate on one corner and the count engraved on the other is B's inversion, paid for in the page's existing vocabulary rather than in a new one: the count leads because it holds an edge, not because it is 34px. That combination needs no class that does not already ship, which is the strongest thing that can be said for a header direction here.

  1. Take A's construction. .lab-slab-fixed for the face, the nameplate for the circle, a second plate on the trailing corner for the count — the trade card's CardLedge row, so the name truncates against the count.
  2. Take B's ranking inside it. The circle's sentence and the rules summary become one caption block under the stepper, so the two grey lines that say overlapping things today become one thing said once.
  3. Leave C's bezel on the card. The gauges are right about captioning every fact and wrong about repeating a housing the row below already owns — the fourth countable depth is allowed there precisely because nothing else in view has one.
  4. D as drawn is not buildable, and D′ is why. D's rail lands in the band the seek key already holds — same offset, same trailing edge, both z-30 — so the choice is never "D or not D", it is whether that key folds into the rail. Folded, it gains an edge for its plate and the board pays for one pinned band instead of two.
  5. D′ is the strongest direction here and the biggest ask. It is a third move for one control in as many revisions, and it should be decided as a question about the seek key rather than as a consequence of picking a header. If the answer is no, D goes with it — and A + B stand on their own, since neither touches a pinned band at all.
  6. Keep E as the measurement, not the plan. It is worth knowing exactly how much the block costs — but the sentence it deletes is the one that explains an inert control, and the bar seat is spoken for.

What none of these change: the scope still leads the search, the search still leads the list, the count is still counted server-side over the two populations, and the whole block stays inside headerRef so the virtualizer is told when it rewraps. TradeControls keeps its props; what changes is what it renders.
