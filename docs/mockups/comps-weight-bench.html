Comps · the comparison weights · four organisations, three seats

Twenty-nine sliders, of which seven are doing anything

The weight bench is the one control on this page whose shape is the answer: which stats are in the comparison, how hard each pulls, and — since windows landed — over what stretch of career each is read. It ships as one row per catalogue field, and that is the thing worth re-deciding, because the catalogue is twenty-nine fields long and a tuned board is about seven of them. Twenty-two rows report that they are switched off.

All four options below draw the identical board — a receiver tuned to targets over the previous three years, receiving yards at his career best, snap share at the best of his last three, and the usual production and age underneath — so the only thing that varies between them is the organisation. Every one is drawn at a laptop width and at 390px, because what a bench costs is mostly height and height only shows up on a phone.

What the bench costs today

Measured, headless Chromium, real faces

780px

The panel's height at a laptop width, with production two-up. On a 390px screen it is 3,800px — ten screens of controls to reach the six fields under Market values.

22 of 29

Rows at weight 0 on a tuned board. Each still spends a label, a slider, a readout and — in production — a window slot, which is the widest control in the row.

16px

The window control has to be set at the iOS zoom floor, so it is the largest type in a row of 12px labels. Twenty of them shouting "This season" is the loudest thing on the panel and the least informative.

Fixable anywhere

The track does not flood to the weight — the thumb is the only cue, so a row reads as "on" only by its rail. Every option below floods it; it costs nothing and belongs in whichever one is built.

None of that is an argument for hiding the catalogue. A reader who wants target share at the career best has to be able to find target share. It is an argument that the board and the catalogue are two different things — the board is what you are comparing on, the catalogue is what you could add — and today they are drawn as one list in which the board is scattered.

What it buys

Nothing is hidden and nothing is a step: a reader who wants a field can see that it exists, what it is called, and that it is currently off. The lit rail down the leading edge is the only thing separating the seven that matter from the twenty-two that don't, and it does read.

It is also the only option where adding a field and weighing it are one gesture — drag from 0 and it is in. Every other option below makes that two.

What it costs

The board is scattered through the catalogue. Targets and Age are eleven rows apart with nine switched-off rows between them, so the thing the reader tuned cannot be read as a unit — which is exactly what the mix strip at the top had to be invented to say.

On a phone it is ten screens, and the window control is drawn twenty times to say "This season" nineteen of them.

What it buys

The board becomes an object rather than a scattering: seven channels side by side, heights comparable at a glance, which is the same information the mix strip carries but on the control itself rather than in a summary above it. This is the option that most looks like the instrument the rest of the app is drawn as.

Height is a far better encoding of a proportion than a horizontal slider in a stack — the eye compares adjacent columns natively, and the tallest channel is the answer to "what is this comparison mostly about".

The panel comes down to ~330px at a laptop width, and the catalogue is one dense tray instead of twenty-two rows.

What it costs

A channel is ~74px wide, so a field's name gets two short lines — "Receiving air yards" and "KTC 90-day trend (SF)" both truncate, and the label is the only thing saying what the fader controls.

The phone is the real cost. Seven channels do not fit 390px, so the rack either scrolls sideways — a horizontal scroller inside a page, which this codebase avoids — or falls back to option A's rows, which makes it two designs either side of a breakpoint.

Adding a field is two gestures: press it in the bin, then raise its fader from zero.

What it buys

It asks the question a reader actually arrives with. Nobody wants targets at 85 rather than 80; they want targets to matter most and age to matter a bit, which is a rank and a coarse magnitude. Five notches reproduce this very board to within a percentage point — the shares beside the notches are 22/22/17/13/13/9/4 against the slider board's 21/21/17/13/13/10/5.

Order carries meaning that the bench throws away: the top row is the comparison's subject. And the per-row share turns the mix strip into something you can read a number off rather than only a picture.

Identical at every width — the row has no control wide enough to force a wrap.

What it costs

It is a change to the model, not just the layout. Weights stay 0–100 on the wire, but a reader who had 85 stored gets snapped to a notch, and fine control is gone for good. That is a real capability removed, and the KNN is sensitive enough that somebody will miss it.

Drag-to-rank is the one interaction here that has no keyboard equivalent without extra work, and it is the primary gesture.

What it buys

It answers the question most readers have, which is not "how much should targets weigh" but "compare them on usage". Four presses replace the whole bench for the common cases, and the sentence under the key states what the current board is doing — including the windows, which is the one thing a tuned board otherwise says nowhere.

It is orthogonal: it sits on top of A, B or C rather than competing with them, and it is the cheapest of the four to build — the presets are four literals and the columns editor's ColumnPreset is the precedent.

What it costs

It does not make the bench smaller; it makes it rarer. Whoever presses Tune still meets whichever of A/B/C is behind it, so this is a layer and never an answer on its own.

Four boards is a claim about what readers want, and a badly chosen set is worse than none — a preset nobody presses is a row of chrome above the control they actually use.

Three seats for the window

The new axis, and where it sits on a row

Whichever organisation wins, the window has to sit somewhere, and it is the control that broke the row's proportions: it is pinned at 16px by the iOS zoom floor, so it is physically the largest thing in a 12px row and it is drawn on every production field whether or not it has been moved.

The third seat is the one worth arguing about, because it questions the premise. Is a window per stat what a reader wants, or one window for the board with exceptions? "Compare them on the last three years" is a sentence about the whole comparison; "targets over three years but snap share this season" is a real question and a rarer one.

S1 · the select

Every window one press away, on the row it belongs to, and the platform gives the keyboard and touch behaviour free. It is the only seat where the reader can see the seven options exist without discovering them.

Costs 136px of every production row and reads "This season" nineteen times.

S2 · the stepper

Two keys and a readout, at 9px — the seat is 96px and the type is free of the zoom floor because nothing is focusable that takes text. The windows are in radius order, so stepping is a walk outward from this season, which is the ordering the catalogue already has.

Costs up to three presses to reach a named window, and the set is invisible until you walk it — the trade the trades board's circle already made deliberately.

S3 · one window, overrides as tags

The bay carries the board's window and a row carries nothing at all unless it differs, where it wears a tag. A default board is one control instead of twenty, and the row goes back to label · slider · readout.

Costs a second concept — per-row override — and a gesture to reach it that none of these mocks draws. It is also the only seat that changes what the prefs store: a board-wide window plus a sparse override map.

What to build

A recommendation, not a vote

D over B, with S3. The boards are the cheapest thing on this page and they answer most of the traffic; the rack is the organisation that makes a tuned board readable as one object, which is the failure the mix strip was papering over; and one window for the bay takes the largest control in the row off nineteen rows that had nothing to say with it.

The honest caveat is that B has a phone problem and D does not fix it — a rack of seven channels cannot be drawn at 390px without either a sideways scroller or a fallback to A's rows. If that is unacceptable, the answer is A with S3 and a parts bin: keep the rows, keep the catalogue in a tray, and the panel comes down to about a third of its height without inventing a layout that only exists on a laptop.

C is the most interesting and the most expensive to be wrong about. It should not be built until somebody has asked whether anyone actually moves a weight to 85 — which is a question the stored boards can answer, and nobody has looked.
